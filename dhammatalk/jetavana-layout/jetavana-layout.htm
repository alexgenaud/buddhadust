Archiologist's Guess As To the Layout of Anāthapiṇḍika's Jeta Grove

The folowing is copied from an AI response on Google. Sources are given but not included here.

Based on textual evidence and decades of archaeological excavations at Saheth (part of the twin historic site Saheth-Maheth near Shravasti in Uttar Pradesh, India), archaeologists have reconstructed the layout of Jetavana Monastery. While the original 5th-century BCE structures built by the wealthy merchant Anathapindika were made of wood and bamboo, centuries of subsequent brick reconstructions—spanning the Kushan, Gupta, and medieval periods—closely preserve the spatial arrangement described in ancient Pali and Chinese Buddhist texts.

Archaeologists map the layout of the Jeta Grove complex through several distinct zones and defining structures:

The Perimeter Walls and Majestic Gateway

The Boundary: Excavations have uncovered an irregular wall surrounding the monastic grounds.

The Main Gate: According to textual accounts and structural foundations, a large, impressive gatehouse designed by Prince Jeta himself stood at the entrance.

The Anandabodhi Tree: Right outside or in front of the main entrance gate sat a sacred Bodhi tree planted by Anathapindika so that devotees could pay respects when the Buddha was away traveling.

The Sacred Inner Core: The Buddha's Chambers The focal point of Jetavana is a pair of foundational shrines situated centrally within the ruins:

Gandhakuti (The Fragrant Chamber): Positioned prominently, this was the personal hut and residence of the Buddha. Archaeological evidence shows a low stone platform and low brick walls remaining from a later two-story brick structure that replaced the original wooden design. It includes a sitting room, bedroom, and steps leading to a presentation area.

Kosambakuti: Located near the Gandhakuti, this served as another key meditation hall used by the Buddha. Excavations revealed a long brick promenade directly in front of it, which corresponds to the Chankama (walking meditation path) used by the Buddha.

The Monastic Living & Administrative Sectors Radiating outwards from the sacred core are the residential clusters and utility buildings:

The Quadrangle Viharas (Monasteries): Archaeologists have unearthed traditional rectangular or square monastery complexes (such as "Monastery No. 19"). These feature a central open courtyard surrounded by rows of individual monastic cells facing inward.

Assembly Halls & Chapter Houses: Large brick pillar foundations indicate spacious, communal multi-pillared pavilions (Mandapas) used for group sermons, legal monastic matters (Uposatha), and gathering.

Utility Zones: The site features numerous scattered ancient brick wells, bathhouses, storerooms, and deep lotus ponds, which align perfectly with the original layout commissioned by Anathapindika to sustain hundreds of monks.

The Commemorative Stupa Belt To the sides and rear of the core area, the landscape is densely dotted with minor brick stupas and votive shrines erected over several centuries by kings and wealthy patrons to honor the Buddha's disciples or mark specific spots where famous discourses were given.
