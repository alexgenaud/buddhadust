SN 4 38.013 Jambukhādaka Saṁyutta: Bhava Sutta Becoming

The Wanderer Rose-apple-eater asks the Venerable Sariputta about abandoning becoming.

The next sutta in his Jambukhādaka Saṁyutta translation is Bhikhu Thanissaro's previously done Dukkha Suttaṁ. where he uses his regular translation of 'dukkha' as "stress" which I think is way too narrow.

From my footnote to this term in my translation:

Dukkha. The dukkha that is dukkha, the dukkha that is saṅkhāra, the dukkha that is vipariṇāma. Dukkha is pain that is known to be pain: physical or mental; the pain of own-making is the pain that results from the identified as personal things that result from identification with the intent to create personal experience through intentional acts of thought, word and deed; the pain of turns of events (vicissitude) is the pain that results from disadvantageous turns of events, things not going the way one expected, changing, ending, natural events that result in sickness, suffering or death. In other words this means ordinary painful things, the pain of being bound to the conseqences of one's deeds, the pain of repeated rebirth in saṁsara.

When the Buddha says: "All things are painful" what he is speaking of is the ultimate situation. When he says there is pleasure found in the world what he is speaking of is the fact that though it is temporary and ends in pain, there is that which is pleasant in this world.

The Maha-Satipaṭṭhana Suttanta gives us a definition of dukkha:
"And what, beggars, is
the Aristocrat of Truths
as to pain?

Birth is pain,
aging is pain,
death is pain.

Grief and lamentation,
pain and misery,
and despair
are pain.

Not to gain the wished for is pain.

Essentially the Five Boundup Stockpiles are pain."

Pāḷi or Sanskrit?

Charles Allen, in Ashoka, casually makes the statement that the two languages arose independently from the Prackrits. This throws out the debate as to which evolved from which and makes the whole picture much clearer. Of course there would have been borrowing back and forth as both would have been known by the educated of the time.

The debate looses interest for me at this juncture (or, rather, disjuncture). What we are reasonably certain of is that the Suttas were first passed along in Pāḷi and then set into writing in Pāḷi.

What I am certain of is that the advice/information given us in the Suttas in the Pāḷi and even in most translations (where the problem of accomplishing the goal comes up at the understanding of how to access Nibbāna which is being confused by the conflation of sankhāra and paccaya which is, at present, only noted by me in my translations — see Is Nibbāna conditioned?) is sufficient for the intelligent and serious to guarantee the accomplishment of the stated goal: the end of pain, the end of rebirth, becoming, the end of being subject to the results of kamma.

Edible Food

Steak
Pad Thai
Fish and Chips
a Hamberger

What are you thinking?

Nutritional value or
Flavor?

How much do you need?

How much time are you actually wasting?

You spend time planning your meals. You spend time making up a shopping list. You spend time driving to the store or stores. You spend time shopping. You spend time transporting the food back home. You spend time arranging the storage of the food. You spend time cooking the food. You spend time eating, chewing, swallowing. Your body takes hours to digest the food. And a day or so later the whole mess is ejected from your system, taking time. And this does not take into consideration of the time you spend at work earning the money to pay for the food you eat.

It is the balance between the time you spend eating versus the time you would spend eating if you ate "just enough to keep the body going, to go a little farther on this way." That's the important thing.

When you are facing death, is it going to be the nutritional value of your food or the flavor that you look at? Which one is more likely to result in regret at losing and which would be more likely the reason you come back to this place again?

"I'm changing so fast
I don't know what's becoming of me."

"The end of becoming is Nibbāna." — Sariputta.

The people who dispute the meaning of bhava as being "becoming" are not looking at this body. There is nothing there that does not change millisecond to millisecond. To say that this living being is not a 'becoming' is not realistic. Of course the word does also mean "living, existing, being, developing' that is not what is in dispute here, it is just the denial that the word means 'becoming.' To see this as 'becoming" is the way that seeing change will eventually bring one to seeing Nibbāna.

Sammā Ajiva: Consummate Lifestyle Consummate Lifestyle is the style or process of living in accordance with the Buddha's system.

Identifying an element of one's lifestyle that is clearly seen by one's self as low, harmful, detrimental to one's self or others one abandons it, lets it go, drops it, renounces it, restrains it, eradicates it.

Some people would have this as "Right Livelihood", but livelihood is covered in the next "Sammā", Sammā Kamanta, Consummate Works (which includes commerse).

Take a look, Sammā Ajiva is talking about a process, a way of living, not the ways one may make a living.

Then we have the situation of this whole group of bhikkhus and laymen telling an inquirer that charging money to act as a Buddhist Chaplan is not theft of Dhamma.

It is theft of Dhamma.

(First off what person or group of persons or institution thinks it knows enough about Dhamma to be issuing certificates approving Buddhist Chaplans?)

But put that aside.

A person asks what he hopes is a group of knowledgable Bhikkhus if it is OK to charge Money for acting as a Buddhist Chaplan.

He is told that it is OK when it is not OK. Let's have a look:

A person goes from a state seeking comforting words from a person he believes understands the Buddha's message. Paying money to the Chaplan or to the institution paying the Chaplan, he gets, let us suppose, some correct advice from the Chaplan and goes away happier than before.

What has happened here?

Vitakka (Applied Thought / Initial Thought)

Vitakka literally means "thinking toward" something. In the context of the first jhāna, it is the mental factor that places and directs the mind onto the meditation object — like a hand picking something up and setting it down. It's the initial application of attention, the act of "touching" the object with the mind.

It's often described with the simile of a bell being struck — vitakka is the initial strike. It counters the hindrance of sloth and torpor by keeping the mind engaged and pointed.

Vicāra (Sustained Thought / Examination)

Vicāra is what happens after vitakka — the mind sustains, explores, and rubs up against the object. It's a gentler, more continuous quality of attention that keeps the mind from drifting away. Continuing the bell simile, vicāra is the resonance that follows the strike.

That is fine as a description of the situation. Let it go at that. Then, in stead of dwelling on that person's actions and the feeling it is creating in one's self, think, instead: "This unpleasant sensation I am experiencing is a consequence of my own previous intention to create unpleasant sensation in another. It is my own kamma returning to me": and abstrain from any reaction by way of thought, word or deed which perpetuates that kammic stream and instead set your mind on the ending of that kammic stream by way of non-reaction. Respond if the situation is seen to require a utilitarian response, but avoid the emotional extra baggage. Avoid too the positive emotinal response as that is better than the hostile, but is still the continuance of the kammic stream and can end up leading to further corruption by the response to that. And avoid also the attempt to escape the unpleasant sensation in some sort of pleasure-causing indulgence.

When experiencing a pleasant sensation from contact with a person the first thought is likely to be something like: "It is proper that this person act like this." OK. That is fine as a description of the situation. Let it go at that. Then, in stead of dwelling on that person's actions and the feeling it is creating in one's self, think, instead: "This pleasant sensation I am experiencing is temporary and could cause me regret at its ending. It is a consequence of my own previous intention to creat pleasant sensation in anoother. It is my own kamma returning to me": and abstain from any reaction by way of thought, word or deed which perpetuates that kammic stream and instead set your mind on the ending of that kammic stream by way of non-reaction. Respond if the situation is seen to require a utilitarian response, but avoid the emotional extra baggage. Avoid too the positive response to the sensation and the fear-caused reaction that could accompany the realizatiion that the positive sensation could end in pain.

When experiencing the absense of either painful sensations or pleasant sensations (adukkha-m-asukha: not, as frequently translated "neutral" sensation, this is the situation where no sensation of pain or pleasure is present. Translatig this term as "neutral" one will miss the freedom that it carries) from contact with a person there is likely to be no thought of that situation at all. This carries with it the danger of acting in some way that starts a new kammic stream and further existence, rebirth, and pain. In stead one should make one's self conscious of the situation by the thought: "This situation wherin there is neither unpleasant sensation nor pleasant sensation, when experienced in connection with the idea of giving up this world of pain, is a small taste of Nibbāna." It is a consequence of my own previous intention to give up attachment. It is my own kamma returning to me: and abstain from any reaction by way of thought word or deed which might result in a new kammic stream. Respond if the situation is seen to require a utilitarian response, but avoid the emotional extra baggage.

Oblog: [O.1.23.26] Friday, January 23, 2026

Anatta

In its constant struggle to find an end to the pain of rebirth the mind of the individual still identifying with this or that goes back and forth along a spectrum that goes from the pleasant to the unpleasant. Back and fourth in a time frame that will vary with individuals.

Identifying something there as the self, in contact with the pleasant or unpleasant, that individual will react with grasping after getting or getting away from. That individual's mind will always think of that grasping as well intentioned whatever its effectiveness may be in the accomplishment of that grasping.

That individual, getting what is considered pleasant, and getting away from what he doesn't want will form the opinion "I am a person who gets what he wants and gets away from what he doesn't want. This is good. Therefore I am a good person."

The problem here is that mistakes will be made because there is identification with this or that, because false information is in the mix, and for a huge number of other reasons. Identifying with some behavior that has got the pleasant or got away from the unpleasant at one point may have the opposite effect at another time.

The result is another layer; another spectrum through which the individual passes, this time with the addition of some formula of denial. "Although I acted with the grasping which ordinarily would have resulted in the desired outcome, it did not have this result because .... so I am still a good guy."

Round and round deeper and deeper into blindness.

The problem here is that that behavior is now no longer seen for what it is: the behavior of an identifying individual (an own-made thing) and is seen as "My response." And that response is guarded and protected by any number of rationalizations so as to make it 'good'.

There is no reaching such a one. Every attempt to correct this problem will be seen as an attack on the individual and will be met with a counter attack. Persisted in, the attempt to correct the individual would only drag the rescuer into the downward spiral of the rescuee.

The way out?

The way out must be accomplished by the person himself. He must recognize the situation as it really is: There is no thing there that is this self that is good or bad, there is only identified-with behaviors that are unpleasant or pleasant in outcome.

The intentions to defend and protect these behaviors must be seen for what they are: defences and protections aimed at supporting identification with behaviors based on opinions which are themselves based on the untrue assumption that a sensation is an aspect of the self.

How does one recognize this situation?

"Like me is like him. What I would not like to experience is what he would not like to experience. So this individuality that I am no longer identifying with will abstain from doing that."

But here it gets deeper: what is seen as "not self" must not only be the unpleasant sensations, the pleasant sensations must also be seen as not self. It is seeing pleasant sensations as self that is the thing that results in seeing self in the first place. Pleasant sensation must be seen as ultimately the driving force that results in pain.

There is no thing there that is the self. So seeing one will no longer behave in ways that are based on grasping. No longer behaving in ways based on grasping there will no longer be defenses and protections of grasping behaviors.

Constipation

This may seem like a strange topic to be discussing on a page supposedly aimed at Buddhist matters ... until you learn of the facts on the ground: Constipation is a very frequent problem among bhikkhus.

I have, fortunately or unfortunately, some experience in the matter and have given it some thought.

First of all, if one follows what the Buddha taught and lives like a beggar, it is difficult to accept the focus on food in most of the reasoning about cause that is found. The beggar has no real choice in the matter. He can refuse but should not be dictating diet. And differentiating foods is something disparaged by the Buddha. Yet most of the discussions on this topic will say constipation is caused by this or that food.

White bread is almost universally cited yet if refined wheat flour were the cause, almost everyone in the world would suffer with this problem.

Other causes cited which make better sense are not consuming enough water; eating little (recommended by the Buddha and prominent bhikkhus, and me); ignoring the need to ... um ... evacuate; little exercise (sitting without walking breaks); change of lifestyle, and stress.

Some of these things often cited as a cause are better thought of, if reversed, as cures as they are things which are freely and easily obtained or done by the beggar: consuming more water, exercising (alternate sitting and walking), attention to the need to 'go"; dealing with stress-causing issues rather than ignoring them or fighting.

Eating little is compensated for by consuming more water and by not worrying about 'regularity". Regularity is a concern faced by people who are regular in their diets. Pay attention to the need, not the timing.

The only thing in that group of causes that cannot be reversed and which is not easily available and free is change of lifestyle. This is both almost universal among bhikkhus and often not possible to control, but is also something that can be counter-balanced by the other cures.

A frequently recommended cure is to eat more fruits and vegetables. (Boil this down and it comes to not consuming enough water.) This is not 100% under the control of the beggar, but is not entirely out of reach either as most givers in society will be including these things in their food donations. In places today, and in most places in older times fruit trees were easily accessible and few would object to the beggar eating fruit that had fallen from the tree. Today it would be a matter of not ignoring or refusing the offerings of fruits and vegetables.

What the beggar then must remember is to not pick and choose, but to eat his vegetables. What is most frequently seen is a smorgasbord offering and the beggars picking and choosing what they like and avoiding what they don't like. The role of the food steward is ignored at a great disadvantage.
